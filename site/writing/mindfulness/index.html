Let's try again: a badly drawn reset button

My hand-drawn comic about getting angry, getting angry at breathing, and trying again. The reset button is a metaphor, not a feature.

A first attempt, and then another

My stick figure tries breathing. Then he gets angry that breathing hasn't fixed his anger. That second part matters.

I drew this comic in English, complete with poorly drawn people and a tiny control-room crew. It gets to the awkward part faster than a definition would: trying to calm down has become one more thing for the character to get frustrated about.




Noticing where my attention went

It's easy for me to get caught up in whatever is happening and forget to ask how I'm feeling. I was skeptical of mindfulness at first. For me, it means pausing to notice my thoughts, feelings and surroundings instead of immediately following them wherever they're going.

That doesn't mean getting rid of difficult emotions. Or never getting distracted. I'm no expert; I still have to remind myself to pay attention.

The pause before trying again

The small thing in this comic isn't the breath count. It's the pause before “Let's try again.” The first attempt hasn't worked the way the character wanted. He notices that he's still caught up in it, and tries once more.

For me, paying closer attention to a conversation or noticing what I need can be part of that. I don't need another list of daily obligations to fail at.

Just breathe

“Just breathe” sounds very simple when you're not the one who's upset. The drawing lets that advice be annoying before it lets the character return and apologize.

There isn't really a chief in my head with a reset button. The comic gets to draw one; real life doesn't owe me that tidy ending. Noticing a feeling doesn't erase it, and a breathing exercise doesn't repair a conversation on its own.

This isn't treatment advice or a promise of relief. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health describes mixed evidence and limitations in mindfulness research, as well as difficult experiences some people report with meditation. Mindfulness isn't a substitute for medical care; treatment decisions belong with a health professional.

Keeping the second attempt

The part I want to keep is “Let's try again.” Not the perfect calm at the end. The chance to notice, come back, and choose what to do next—even after the first attempt was mostly being angry at breathing.
